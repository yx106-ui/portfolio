# Let's Fly Now — Redesigning Event Sign-Ups for the Ninety-Nines

**Role:** UX/UI Designer (one of two designers on a cross-functional volunteer team, via Develop for Good)
**Client:** The Ninety-Nines, Inc. — the international organization of women pilots founded in 1929, with Amelia Earhart as its first president. Let's Fly Now is their First Flight program introducing women and girls to aviation through hands-on flight experiences led by local chapters.
**Team:** 7 people via Develop for Good — one product manager, one technical manager, two designers (including me), and three backend engineers
**Timeline:** Oct 2025 – Feb 2026
**Tools:** Figma (low- and high-fidelity prototypes)
**My focus:** Landing page redesign · Sign-up flows and form pages for pilots, ground volunteers, and passengers

> **TL;DR:** The Ninety-Nines ran their Let's Fly Now events on paper sign-up forms — data was collected inconsistently and regularly lost. As the student designer responsible for the landing page and all three sign-up flows, I designed a digital system that turns every participant type's registration into a structured, recoverable dataset, while making the program's entry point clearer and more welcoming.

## The Problem: Data That Lived on Paper

Let's Fly Now events involve three kinds of participants — volunteer pilots, ground volunteers, and passengers (the women and girls taking their first flight). Historically, all of them signed up on paper forms at events.

The consequences, as the organization described them to us:

- Sign-up data was **collected inconsistently** across chapters and events
- Records were **never reliably aggregated** — and some data was simply **lost**
- The organization couldn't follow up with participants, track volunteer history, or report on program reach

This wasn't a "make it prettier" project. The redesign had a concrete operational goal: **move sign-ups from paper to structured digital forms so the data survives.**

---

## Understanding the Client

This was my first project with a real external client, and the discovery phase taught me things a classroom brief never could:

- **The stated request is rarely the whole need.** The organization asked for a website redesign; what they needed was a data pipeline. Getting there required asking about their workflows — what happens to a paper form after an event? Who types it up? (Often: no one.)
- **Nonprofits design for volunteers, not employees.** Event organizers are chapter volunteers with varying technical comfort. Every flow had to work for someone running an event for the first time, with no training.
- **Constraints are design inputs.** Our pages had to live inside the Ninety-Nines' existing website (ninety-nines.org), not replace it. That meant studying their current design language — navigation structure, typography, color, and tone — and designing new pages that feel native to a site I didn't create. Matching an existing system faithfully, rather than imposing my own aesthetic, was a discipline this project forced me to build.

We ran a weekly cadence throughout the project: a 45-minute meeting with the Ninety-Nines to surface needs and gather feedback, plus a 45-minute internal team debrief to align on what we'd heard and update our direction. That rhythm mattered — requirements didn't arrive fully formed in a brief; they emerged over weeks of conversation, and the internal debriefs were where we translated what the client said into what we'd actually design.

## My Contributions

### 1. Landing page redesign

I redesigned the Let's Fly Now landing page as the program's front door: a clear description of the program, a testimonial from a participant, and immediate paths into each of the three roles — *Join as a Pilot*, *Join as a Passenger*, *Join as a Ground Volunteer* — plus an event-organizer path with a step-by-step "Event Actions" guide (create an event → request insurance → order logbooks) and an FAQ section for common questions.

[IMAGE: Landing page — full scroll, in device mockup]

### 2. Sign-up flows for all three participant types

I wireframed and designed the detail pages and forms for pilots, ground volunteers, and passengers. Each role has different needs:

- **Pilots** — a returning-pilot login vs. new pilot sign-up split, collecting personal information plus required credentials: pilots upload materials like insurance and license documentation, since flying passengers demands verification the paper system handled poorly
- **Ground volunteers** — a role explanation ("the backbone of Let's Fly Now") with volunteer stories to encourage sign-ups, leading into a simple form
- **Passengers** — a sign-up form with conditional logic: if the passenger is under 18, the form requires guardian information. Many first-flight participants are girls, so guardian consent wasn't an edge case — it was a core requirement, and exactly the kind of conditional data a paper form captures unreliably

Each flow ends in a confirmation state, so participants know their registration was recorded — the exact assurance the paper system couldn't give anyone.

### 3. Storytelling as a recruitment tool

A small design decision I'm fond of: each role page includes real stories — a pilot describing why volunteering reminded her why she loves aviation, a ground volunteer recalling a scared first-time flyer telling her "you helped me feel ready." These aren't decoration; for a volunteer-powered program, the sign-up page is also a persuasion page.

## Working in a Design Team

Two things I took from this project beyond the designs themselves:

1. **Learning by pairing.** My teammate taught me their approach to user flow diagramming, which I then applied to map all three sign-up journeys before designing a single screen. Designing the flow first meant the form pages almost designed themselves.
2. **Owning a slice of a shared system — inside a cross-functional team.** As one of two designers working alongside a PM, a technical manager, and three backend engineers, my pages had to feel continuous with my design partner's work *and* be buildable within what the engineers were implementing — same navigation, same components, same voice, and forms whose fields mapped to the data structure the backend actually needed. Negotiating a shared design language across both designers and engineers is a different skill than inventing one alone, and one I'm glad to have practiced before entering a professional team.

---

## Outcome & Reflection

We presented the design at the Develop for Good batch showcase and handed off our prototypes and design recommendations to the Ninety-Nines. The organization works with a professional design firm for implementation, so our role was to deliver tested design direction — the prototypes, user flows, and rationale — as concrete input for that work. Handing off to another design team turned out to be its own lesson: the deliverable isn't just screens, it's the *reasoning*, documented clearly enough that people who never met us can build on it.

What this project taught me, in one line: **understanding a client's operations is a design skill.** The best screens I made came not from visual exploration but from asking what happens to a piece of paper after the event ends.
